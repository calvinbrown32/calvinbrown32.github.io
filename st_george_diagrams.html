Modeling Complex Transit Environments for Accessible Navigation and Wayfinding

This project develops GTFS Pathways and Apple’s Indoor Mapping Data Format (IMDF) data models of Staten Island’s St. George Ferry Terminal to demonstrate how standardized spatial and navigational data can provide a shared foundation for routing applications, accessible passenger navigation, and the production of architectural and wayfinding graphics.

About This Project

GTFS Pathways and Apple’s Indoor Mapping Data Format (IMDF) provide standardized ways to represent the layout, features, and navigational connections of complex, multilevel, multimodal transit facilities. By providing structured, machine-readable data, these specifications create a shared foundation for architectural and wayfinding design, passenger information systems, and indoor navigation applications.

This project demonstrates how these data models can support applications beyond conventional station maps and diagrams. Developers can use the data to build routing tools that guide passengers through a terminal, from its entrances to individual platforms and ferry slips. With accurate accessibility information and up-to-date operational data, these tools could also help passengers with mobility needs navigate around elevator outages and other disruptions.

I extended this demonstration by integrating real-time transit data APIs to display upcoming departures, illustrating how indoor mapping and navigation data can be combined with live transit information to provide passengers with more timely and actionable guidance. The same underlying data can also support the production and updating of maps, diagrams, and other passenger information products.

Using the St. George Ferry Terminal as a case study, this project explores how standardized transit and indoor mapping data can connect spatial information, live transit services, architectural design, wayfinding, and accessible passenger navigation.

Methodology

The GTFS Pathways and IMDF datasets presented here are proof-of-concept models developed for demonstration purposes. They have not been independently verified and should not be considered authoritative representations of the terminal.

To develop the models, I used QGIS to georeference publicly available drawings of the St. George Ferry Terminal. I then used Claude AI to assist in interpreting the drawings and extracting their spatial and navigational information. I provided the AI with a structured list of target features, including relevant spaces, connections, and other elements needed to represent the terminal in the two data models.

I reviewed the extracted information and supplied annotated versions of the drawings to clarify features and relationships that required further interpretation. This iterative process produced a detailed representation of the information depicted in the source drawings, which I used to develop the demonstration GTFS Pathways and IMDF datasets.

The resulting models illustrate how georeferenced architectural drawings and AI-assisted information extraction can help translate complex indoor environments into structured data for mapping, navigation, and wayfinding applications. Further verification against authoritative plans and on-site conditions would be necessary before using the datasets in operational applications.
